How an LLM works

One level up from the architecture: how a next-token predictor becomes something you can hand a codebase to.

The previous document covered the machine. This one covers everything that turns that machine into an assistant: how it's trained, what it's actually optimising, what happens on each turn of a conversation, and where the model ends and the scaffolding around it begins.

Worth stating up front, because it explains more downstream behaviour than anything else: a language model is a stateless function. It has no memory, no session, no internal notepad that persists. Every apparent continuity — a conversation that remembers your name, an agent working through a long task — is produced by replaying the entire history into a fresh call.

Pretraining

Take an enormous corpus of text. Show the model a passage with a token hidden, ask it to predict that token, measure how wrong it was, and nudge every weight slightly in the direction that would have been less wrong. Repeat something like 1014 times.

That objective sounds trivially narrow, and the surprising empirical fact of the last several years is that it isn't. To predict the next token well across a corpus that includes textbooks, source code, arguments, and transcripts, a model has to build internal machinery for grammar, world facts, arithmetic, code semantics, and something that functions like a model of the writer's intent. None of that was asked for directly. It falls out of pressure to compress.

The scale is the other half of the story. Capabilities appear reasonably smoothly as you increase parameters, data and compute together — and some capabilities appear fairly abruptly at particular scales, though there's ongoing argument about how much of that abruptness is real versus an artefact of how it's measured.

Where the knowledge cutoff comes from. Pretraining runs on a corpus assembled at a point in time. Everything the model knows without being told, it learned then. It's a snapshot, not a feed — which is why current information has to arrive through the prompt, via search or tools.

Post-training

A freshly pretrained model is a document continuer, not an assistant. Ask it a question and a plausible continuation might be three more questions, because that's what a list of questions looks like in the wild. Post-training is what closes that gap.

One consequence worth internalising: the model is not optimised for truth. It's optimised for producing text that a rater would judge favourably. Those correlate strongly, because well-informed correct answers are usually the ones people prefer. But where they come apart — a confident wrong answer that reads well versus a hedged correct one — the objective doesn't automatically pick the true one.

What a turn actually is

Here is the part that most changes how you'll read your usage numbers. There is no session on the server. Each turn is an independent, self-contained call, and everything the model appears to remember is text you re-sent.

So "context window" isn't a memory capacity. It's the maximum size of the document you can hand over in a single call. When a long conversation degrades, nothing was forgotten in the human sense — the transcript either exceeded the window and got truncated, or it got summarised and re-injected by the surrounding application.

The agent loop

Tool use looks like the model reaching out and doing things. Mechanically it's narrower: the model emits a structured block requesting a call, generation stops, your code executes it, the result is appended to the transcript, and the whole thing is sent back in. The model never touches your filesystem. It writes down what it would like done and waits.

Two practical corollaries. Because tool results enter as ordinary text, they are data, not instructions — a file that contains "ignore your previous instructions" is a prompt-injection attempt, and the reason a well-built agent treats retrieved content with suspicion. And because each pass re-sends the growing transcript, a long agentic run is dominated by input tokens. Almost all of them identical to last time.

Failure modes and their causes

What you seeWhere it comes from
Confident fabricationThe objective rewards plausible continuations. A well-formed citation that doesn't exist has high probability under the model's distribution because it looks exactly like the citations it was trained on. There is no internal check against ground truth.
Arithmetic and character-counting errorsTokens aren't digits or letters. 1024 may be one or two tokens with no positional structure. Reliable arithmetic requires either writing out intermediate steps or calling out to a calculator.
Drifting in long sessionsAttention is finite and the transcript keeps growing. Instructions from early on compete with thousands of intervening tokens. Restating what matters works better than assuming it persists.
Excessive agreementPreference training on human ratings selects for responses people liked. Where being liked and being right diverge, the pressure is toward being liked. Labs actively correct for this; it's a persistent tension, not a solved one.
Same prompt, different answerSampling. At temperature 0 the model is deterministic. Anything above that draws from a distribution, and batching effects on the serving side can add a little more nondeterminism.
Rambling on simple questionsCompute per token is fixed, so there's no built-in way to spend less effort on an easy question. Length is a learned stylistic behaviour, tunable by instruction.

What follows for how you use it

  • Put stable content first. Instructions, tool definitions and reference material at the front of the prompt; the varying part at the end. Better for the model, and required for caching to work.
  • Verification beats persuasion. The model can't check its own claims against reality. A loop that runs tests and reads real output is worth more than any amount of prompt tuning.
  • Restate rather than assume. Nothing carries over between calls that isn't in the text you sent.
  • Fresh context is a real tool. When a session has accumulated failed attempts and dead ends, starting over with a clean spec often outperforms trying to correct course in place.
